What 100% Silicone Does for a San Joaquin Valley Roof

Silicone earns its place here for three reasons that map directly onto Visalia's weather. First, UV: silicone is essentially unaffected by ultraviolet exposure — it does not chalk away meaningfully, embrittle, or degrade under the Valley sun the way acrylics and asphalt products do. In a place with 260-plus days of sun and summer roof temperatures north of 150°F, that is the headline property.

Second, ponding water. Flat roofs around Visalia pond — decades of settling, added equipment, and clogged drains guarantee it — and most coatings fail under standing water. Acrylics are water-based and will soften and re-emulsify in a pond. Silicone is a moisture-cure chemistry that is unbothered by permanent ponding; it is the coating category insurers and manufacturers accept over ponded areas. Given tule fog season, when a roof can stay wet for a week, that tolerance is not a luxury.

Third, reflectivity. A white silicone surface reflects the majority of solar radiation, dropping rooftop temperatures dramatically. That cuts cooling load in the building below, extends the life of rooftop HVAC equipment that no longer breathes 150°F air, and helps roof-surface requirements under California's Title 24 cool-roof provisions when work rises to the level of an alteration.

There is also a quieter benefit that Visalia building owners feel at budget season: predictability. A coated roof's future is a known quantity — periodic inspections, drain housekeeping, and a recoat at $2–$4 per square foot somewhere in the 10–20 year window. Compare that to the membrane-roof future of escalating repairs followed by a six-figure tear-off, and the coating is as much a financial instrument as a waterproofing one. Facilities managers who have to defend capital plans tend to become coating advocates quickly.

These are not abstract properties; they map to line items. The reflectivity shows up on summer power bills and in HVAC service intervals. The ponding tolerance shows up as warranty coverage that doesn't carve out the middle of your roof. The UV stability shows up as a coating still doing its job in year twelve instead of chalking away in year six. When we say silicone is the default chemistry for this valley, that is the ledger we are reading from — twenty years of watching coatings age on roofs we drive past weekly.

Pros and Cons of Silicone Roof Coating

Owners deserve the whole ledger, so here it is — both columns.

  • Pro — no tear-off: the existing roof stays, embodied cost and all; the coating renews the surface. Total project cost typically runs $2–$4 per square foot versus multiples of that for replacement.
  • Pro — seamless and self-flashing: applied as a liquid, silicone flows into seams, around penetrations, and over flashings, eliminating the joints where flat roofs actually leak.
  • Pro — UV and ponding immunity: the two conditions that dominate Valley roof failure are the two conditions silicone handles best.
  • Pro — renewable: at the end of its 10–20 year cycle, the roof is cleaned and recoated. The cycle repeats. There is no end-of-life tear-off looming.
  • Con — slick when wet: uncoated silicone is slippery under fog or rain; we embed granules in walkways and around equipment for traction.
  • Con — adhesion is unforgiving of bad prep: silicone bonds to clean, sound, dry substrates and almost nothing bonds to silicone. Skip the wash, the primer where needed, or the moisture survey, and the coating fails. This is a workmanship trade — hire accordingly.
  • Con — not a structural fix: coating over saturated insulation or a rotten deck buries a problem. Wet material must be found (that is what core sampling is for), cut out, and replaced first.
  • Con — future recoats want silicone: once a roof is silicone, its next coat is silicone too. Since silicone is the top-performing chemistry here anyway, we consider this a footnote, but it is real.

If a roof is not a good silicone candidate — too far gone, too wet, structurally compromised — the honest answer is a different scope, sometimes a new spray polyurethane foam roof over the top, and occasionally a replacement. The free assessment exists so that determination is made with data instead of a sales quota.

Timing the decision is its own small art. The cheapest moment to coat a roof is a year or two before it obviously needs it — while the substrate is still dry and the repair line in the bid is still small. Owners who wait for the first interior leak often pay for wet-insulation replacement that a proactive recoat would have prevented entirely. If your roof is past its tenth year and nobody has assessed it since installation, that is the trigger: get the free survey done this season, even if the coating waits for next season's budget.

Is Your Roof a Candidate for Restoration?

The candidacy test is condition, not age. A twenty-five-year-old built-up roof that is dry and adhered is a better coating candidate than a ten-year-old TPO roof holding water in its insulation. Our assessment answers the question empirically: the drone survey maps the whole surface — ponding, patches, blisters, seam conditions, rusted panels — and core samples tell us what is happening inside the assembly where it matters. Within 48 hours you get a written bid stating what the roof is, what percentage of it needs repair before coating, and what the finished system costs.

As broad guidance from twenty years of Visalia roofs: metal roofs are almost always candidates unless rust has perforated panels. Foam roofs are nearly always candidates — recoating is their designed maintenance path. Built-up and mod-bit roofs are usually candidates once wet sections are replaced. Single-ply membranes are case-by-case, hinging on how the seams and the insulation below have fared. Roofs with chronic structural deflection or widespread saturation are not candidates, and we will say so in writing.

One more candidacy note specific to this market: buildings change hands in Visalia's commercial corridors, and buyers routinely inherit roofs with no history at all. A pre-purchase roof assessment is one of the cheapest pieces of due diligence available — the drone survey and cores tell you within 48 hours whether you are buying a roof with a $3-per-foot future or a $12-per-foot one, and that difference has renegotiated more than one escrow. We run these for buyers, sellers, and lenders on the same free-assessment footing.

Silicone Over Spray Foam: How the Recoat Cycle Works

A large share of our coating work is the scheduled renewal of SPF roofs — many of them roofs we installed. Foam roofing is designed around this exact maintenance model: the foam provides insulation and waterproofing and essentially does not age while protected; the silicone coating takes the UV, the ag dust abrasion, and the foot traffic, then gets renewed every 10–20 years at $2–$4 per square foot. The building never experiences a tear-off again.

A recoat visit is straightforward: wash the roof, cut out and re-foam any incidental damage (a gouge from a dropped tool, a hail bruise, a spot where a trade cut in carelessly), re-detail anything questionable, and spray fresh silicone to the specified millage. A day or three of work for most buildings, no interruption below, and the clock resets. Owners who inherit a foam roof and are unsure where it sits in its cycle can start with the same free assessment — coating thickness and condition tell us everything, and the economics are laid out plainly in our spray foam roofing cost guide.

Metal Roof Coating for Packing Houses, Shops, and Warehouses

Tulare County may have more metal roofing per capita than anywhere else we could operate, and Visalia's ring of packing houses, equipment sheds, mini-storage, and light industrial buildings supplies a steady stream of the same complaint: fastener leaks. Thermal cycling through 100-degree summers backs screws out and cooks their gaskets; lap seams open; rust follows the fog. Re-screwing and caulking buys seasons, not solutions.

A silicone metal-roof system solves the failure points categorically. Every fastener head gets encapsulated — brushed or sprayed individually with mastic-grade silicone — every lap seam gets reinforced, rust gets converted or treated, and the whole roof is then coated monolithically. The panels stay, the leaks stop, and the white surface drops the summer heat load on buildings that usually have minimal insulation. For packing operations in Exeter and Woodlake, we schedule this work in the off-season so the roof is tight before harvest; for occupied shops along the 99 corridor in Goshen, the work proceeds without the tenants stopping.

Where a metal building needs insulation as well as waterproofing, the better system is often foam over the metal with silicone over the foam — the full assembly described on our installation page. The assessment tells us which building you have.

Metal-roof owners also ask about color and heat. The standard answer for this valley is white or near-white: the reflectivity gain against a weathered galvanized or dark-painted panel is enormous, and on an uninsulated shop the difference is felt on the floor within a day of completion. Where a business wants a brand color or a planning context calls for something quieter than bright white, tinted silicones exist and we spray them — with the honest note that darker tints give back some of the cooling benefit, a trade-off we quantify rather than hand-wave.

Ponding Water, UV, and the Two Seasons of Roof Abuse

It bears repeating as its own subject, because it is the core of the Visalia case for silicone: this valley alternates between the two conditions that kill roof coatings, and silicone is the chemistry indifferent to both. Summer delivers four-plus months of extreme UV and rooftop temperatures that turn lesser coatings chalky and brittle. Winter delivers tule fog — day after day of saturated, non-drying dampness — plus the ponding that follows every rain on a low-slope roof with imperfect drainage.

Acrylic coatings, the budget alternative, are respectable products in dry-fast climates and the wrong products here: water-based, they erode measurably every year under UV and cannot tolerate ponding without softening. Asphalt-based coatings age even faster in the sun. Silicone's moisture-cure chemistry actually uses ambient humidity to cure, shrugs at ponds, and holds its properties under UV for its full cycle. We stock and spray other chemistries when a substrate demands it, but in twenty years of Visalia weather, 100% silicone has been the default for a reason.

From Wash to Final Walk: How a Coating Project Runs

A restoration project in Visalia follows a sequence that rarely surprises anyone by the time it starts, because the written bid already described it. Day one is almost always washing — a pressure wash that pulls off the season's ag dust, the winter's organic debris, and the chalk of whatever the old surface has been shedding. On a Valley roof this step is genuinely substantial; skipping or rushing it is the single most common cause of coating failure industry-wide, which is why we treat wash day as seriously as spray day.

Repairs come next: wet insulation found by the core samples is cut out and replaced, open seams get reinforced with mastic and fabric, fastener heads on metal roofs are individually encapsulated, blisters are cut and healed, and questionable flashings are re-detailed. This is the stage where the roof's real condition — already documented in your assessment — gets corrected rather than buried. Only a dry, sound, clean surface gets coated; that rule has no exceptions on our crews.

Then the silicone goes on, typically in two coats of contrasting colors. The contrast is our built-in quality control: any spot where the finish color shows thin reveals the base coat beneath it, so full coverage is verifiable by eye and by wet-millage gauge as the work proceeds. Granules are broadcast into designated walkways and around rooftop equipment while the final coat is wet, giving service techs traction for the decade ahead.

The final walk happens with you or your facilities contact on the roof if you want to be there — thickness readings, detail checks, photos, and the paperwork for your records. Most Visalia projects run from a few days to a week or two depending on size and repair scope, and because the work is staged from a shop minutes away, weather interruptions cost days, not mobilization fees.

Silicone vs. Acrylic vs. Urethane

Owners comparing bids will meet all three words, so here is the honest taxonomy. Silicone: best UV stability, only mainstream chemistry rated for permanent ponding, moisture-cure (fog doesn't frighten it), premium price within the coating world, slick when wet (granules fix this). Acrylic: cheapest, easy to apply, genuinely fine on steep metal in mild climates; erodes under Valley UV and fails under ponding — we rarely specify it here. Urethane: toughest against physical abrasion and foot traffic, common as a base coat in high-traffic details or under silicone topcoats; less UV-stable alone. Many of our systems use urethane where gear gets serviced and silicone everywhere else — chemistry by zone, not by slogan.

What matters more than the chemistry argument is millage and prep: a properly washed roof with wet material replaced and coating applied at spec thickness will outperform a premium product slapped thin over dust every single time. That is why our bids state thickness, and why our two-coat contrasting-color method exists — the second coat visibly reveals any spot the first missed.

Millage — the applied thickness of the coating — deserves its own sentence in any comparison, because it is where low bids hide. Two contractors quoting “a silicone roof coating” can be describing systems that differ by half the material: one specifying finished thickness verified by gauge, the other spraying until the color looks even. Thickness is what gets consumed over the service life; thin systems simply run out of coating early. Every Allstate bid states the specified millage in writing, and the two-coat contrasting-color method plus wet-film gauging during application is how we prove the roof got it. When you compare our number against another, compare that line first.
