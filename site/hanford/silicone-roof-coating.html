Silicone Roof Coating in Hanford: Restoration Instead of Replacement

Silicone roof coating gives Hanford building owners a way out of the most expensive sentence in commercial property ownership: "the roof needs to be replaced." In most cases, it doesn't. If the deck is sound and the existing roof — foam, built-up, modified bitumen, single-ply, or metal — is aging rather than structurally failed, a fluid-applied 100% silicone system restores it to watertight, reflective, warrantable condition for $2–$4 per square foot instead of the $5–$12 that tear-off and re-roofing runs. For the packing houses, warehouses, county buildings, and Highway 198 corridor businesses that make up Hanford's commercial stock, that difference is frequently six figures.

The logic is simple: most "failed" roofs in Kings County aren't failed structures, they're failed surfaces. Twenty summers of Valley sun cooks the top layer — asphalt alligators, membranes chalk, coatings thin out — while the assembly underneath is still doing its job. Silicone replaces that spent surface with a seamless, UV-immune, ponding-proof skin that bonds to what's there and weathers in its place. It's the same chemistry we use as the protective topcoat on our spray foam roofs, applied here as a restoration system in its own right. Family-owned, licensed (Lic #C-2-1052735), BBB accredited, SPFA member, and 35 minutes from Hanford via CA-198 — we've been coating South Valley roofs for more than twenty years.

What "100% Silicone" Actually Means

Coatings are not interchangeable, and the label matters. We install high-solids, 100% silicone coatings — meaning the wet film you pay for cures into nearly the same thickness of dry rubber, rather than losing a third of its volume to evaporating carriers. Compare that with economy acrylics, which are water-based: they lose significant film build as they dry, they re-soften under standing water, and they wear measurably every year under UV and dust scour.

Silicone's chemistry is inorganic at its backbone, which is why the San Joaquin Valley's most punishing input — ultraviolet radiation — essentially doesn't age it. It stays flexible from freezing fog mornings to 150°F afternoon decks. It is moisture-cure, meaning humidity helps rather than hinders curing. And critically for Kings County's dead-flat roofs, it is unaffected by ponding water: where acrylics blister and re-emulsify under a week-long winter pond, silicone simply doesn't care. When we say the words "100% silicone" in a Hanford bid, that's the entire performance story in three words.

Millage is where coating bids quietly diverge, so ask every contractor the same two questions: what solids content, and what finished dry-film thickness? A high-solids silicone applied at the specified gallons per square delivers the film the warranty math assumes. A thinned product, or the right product spread too far, looks identical the day it's done and fails years early. We state target wet and dry millage in the bid, apply with calibrated equipment, verify with thickness gauges as we go, and record the readings in your closeout documents — so the number you paid for is the number that's actually on the roof.

Third, the energy math. A fresh white silicone surface reflects the large majority of solar energy that a gray, weathered roof was absorbing. In a town with a hundred-plus days of cooling load, dropping the roof surface temperature by 50 degrees or more shows up on the PG&E bill the very first summer, and it helps alteration projects pencil under California's Title 24 cool-roof provisions without redesigning the assembly.

Silicone Flat Roof Coating and the Ponding Problem

Hanford was laid out on some of the flattest ground in California, and its roofs match the terrain. Dead-flat decks plus winter rain plus tule fog that slows evaporation for days equals ponding — the standing water that kills more Valley flat roofs than any other single cause. Water sitting on a seamed membrane finds the laps. Water sitting on aged asphalt soaks into the alligatoring. Water sitting on a clogged drain field freezes on fog-season nights and pries surfaces apart.

Silicone flat roof coating is the only major coating chemistry that's flatly warrantable under permanent ponding. It doesn't re-emulsify, doesn't blister from moisture drive, and doesn't lose adhesion under standing water. On chronic-pond roofs we go further: because we're also a spray foam company, we can build foam crickets and slope corrections in the low spots before coating, converting a birdbath deck into one that drains. That combination — spray polyurethane foam for geometry, silicone for surface — is a fix that a coatings-only contractor simply can't offer.

Wind-blown ag dust makes ponding worse than the rainfall numbers suggest. Grit washes to the low spots and drains, forms dams, and deepens the ponds year over year. Our maintenance schedules for Hanford roofs include drain-field checks timed to the post-harvest dust season, because a coating system is only as good as the drainage it sits on.

One more Hanford-specific note on drainage: check your scuppers after harvest. The same weeks that fill the air with dust fill parapet scuppers with it, and the first November storm meets a dammed outlet. Ten minutes with a shop broom in late October has saved more Valley flat roofs than any product anyone sells.

The Pros and Cons of Silicone — Straight Answers

Every material has trade-offs, and you should hear silicone's from the people who install it. The pros, concentrated:

  • UV immunity. Valley sun doesn't meaningfully degrade silicone — the single most important property on a 150°F Hanford roof.
  • Ponding resistance. Warrantable under standing water; the decisive advantage on Kings County's dead-flat decks.
  • Seamless application. Fluid-applied, it flashes penetrations and details as part of the field.
  • Renewability. When the coating wears, you clean and recoat — the roof never goes back to zero.
  • Reflectivity. High solar reflectance cuts cooling loads and supports Title 24 compliance.

And the cons, stated plainly:

  • Slick when wet. Cured silicone is slippery in rain or fog; we embed granules in walkway paths and around equipment on roofs with regular foot traffic.
  • Dust pickup. Fresh silicone holds airborne dust more than some surfaces — real in Hanford's ag environment. It dulls the bright white over time, which is cosmetic, and periodic cleaning restores most reflectivity.
  • Recoat loyalty. Almost nothing adheres to silicone except more silicone — a non-issue if you maintain the system, a constraint if you later want a different chemistry.
  • Not a structural fix. Coating restores surfaces; it cannot rescue a saturated or structurally failed roof. Honest assessment has to come first, and ours does.

Metal Roof Coating for Ag and Industrial Steel

Metal roof coating may be the highest-return version of this entire service in Kings County, because the county is carpeted in steel: pre-engineered shops and warehouses in Hanford's industrial blocks, gin and processing structures down Highway 43 toward Corcoran, equipment barns on every section of farmland, and the commercial steel that serves Lemoore and Avenal. Metal roofs here fail at their details — fastener gaskets baked dead by decade two, panel laps opened by thermal cycling, rust tracking along seams where fog condensation sits — long before the panels themselves are done.

Our metal restoration sequence attacks exactly those details: pressure wash and prep, rust conversion on oxidized areas, seam and fastener treatment — every screw head individually encapsulated or brush-sealed — then a full-field 100% silicone application at specified millage. The result is a monolithic waterproof skin over the entire panel system, reflective white instead of heat-soaking galvanized or faded paint, with every one of the ten thousand fasteners that used to be a future leak sealed under rubber.

Two Hanford-specific notes. First, condensation: coating the exterior doesn't stop interior-side panel sweating in fog season — that's an insulation problem, and our commercial spray foam service handles it from the underside; the two treatments together fully rehabilitate a steel building envelope. Second, heat: an un-coated metal roof radiates brutal heat into shop space all summer, and owners consistently report the workspace itself feels different the first week after a white silicone coat goes on.

Gin and grain structures down the 43 corridor bring their own texture: tall corrugated surfaces, decades of galvanic wear, and dust loads heavier than anything in town. The math still favors coating — a gin roof's enormous, simple area is exactly where per-square-foot savings multiply — but prep is the project. Expect more washing, more rust conversion, and honest talk about any panels past saving. Steel that's structurally sound but cosmetically rough is silicone's ideal patient; steel that's perforated needs repair first, and the assessment will say so plainly.

Recoating SPF Roofs: The 10–20 Year Cycle

If your building already has a foam roof — and plenty of Kings County ag facilities do — silicone recoating is the maintenance act that makes it permanent. SPF roofs age from the topcoat down: the silicone weathers slowly, thinning over 10–20 years, while the foam beneath stays protected. Recoat before the foam is ever exposed and the cycle resets; the system's documented lifespan then extends past four decades, as covered in how long does spray foam roofing last.

Our recoat process on foam: clean, inspect the entire field, repair any impact damage or blisters — the fix procedures live on our foam roof repair page — then re-apply silicone to full specified thickness. At $2–$4 per square foot on a 10-to-20-year interval, it's among the cheapest per-year roofing costs any commercial building can achieve. We put every Hanford foam roof we touch on a documented recoat schedule so the interval is tracked by us, not by whoever notices a leak first.

Between recoats, the system wants an occasional look. We recommend a walk-through after the first storms and again after the summer dust season — checking drains, walkway granules, and any spot where equipment trades have been working. Coating damage from a careless HVAC repair is the most common thing we find up there, and it's a twenty-minute fix when caught the same season it happened.

Fog, Dust, and 150°F: The Local Conditions That Choose the Coating

Material selection is climate-driven, and Kings County's climate makes the choice unusually clear-cut. Summer delivers months of extreme UV and roof surfaces at 150°F — conditions that age organic coatings fast and favor silicone's inorganic backbone. Winter delivers tule fog: near-permanent surface moisture for days at a stretch, which punishes water-sensitive acrylics during both application windows and service life, while moisture-cure silicone is unbothered. The dust load off surrounding fields scours all surfaces, favoring high-build systems with granule reinforcement in traffic areas. And the daily thermal swing — a fog-chilled morning to a blistering afternoon — demands the elasticity silicone keeps across that whole range.

This is also why application timing is a local skill. We schedule Hanford coating work around fog-season dew points and afternoon wind events, verify substrate moisture before spraying, and adjust cure windows to the season. A coating spec written for San Diego fails in the basin; ours are written for the basin.

Foot-traffic planning is part of the spec, not a change order. Roofs with monthly filter-change routes get granulated walkway paths from the hatch to every serviced unit; roofs with rare access can run smooth. Getting this right on day one is cheaper than retrofitting paths after a maintenance tech discovers wet silicone's slickness the hard way.

What a Hanford Coating Project Actually Looks Like

Owners picture roofing projects as weeks of disruption; a coating restoration rarely is. A typical 20,000-square-foot Hanford roof runs about a week end to end. Day one is wash-down — pressure washing the field, flushing drains, and letting the Valley's dry air do what it does best. Day two is surgery: cutting out the wet spots the cores flagged, re-foaming or patching them, treating rust on metal, and priming where the substrate chemistry asks for it. Days three through five are application — details and penetrations brush-flashed first, then the field coat sprayed in measured passes, then granules embedded in the walkway routes while the final coat is still wet.

Site logistics stay light. The crew stages from a corner of your yard or an agreed lane of the parking lot; there's no dumpster train because there's no tear-off, and no days-long roar of demolition. Overspray control is a discipline we take as seriously downtown as beside a neighbor's equipment yard — wind screens, masking, and a spray plan that respects the day's forecast. Your operation keeps running underneath the entire time; most tenants notice the smell of a freshly washed roof more than anything else.

Closeout is paper, and it's the part most contractors skip. You receive drone imagery of the finished field, millage readings by area, the products and batch records used, the warranty terms, and the maintenance schedule with its two seasonal check-ins marked. File it with the building documents: when the property refinances, sells, or hits its recoat window in the 2040s, that folder answers every question anyone will ask about this roof.
