How Kings County Weather Breaks Roofs

Hanford's climate attacks roofs on a schedule. Summer is the slow kill: months of 100°F-plus air and roof surfaces near 150°F cook the oils out of asphalt, chalk single-ply membranes, and cycle every seam and fastener through a daily expansion-contraction workout. By September, the roof is brittle, micro-cracked, and primed to fail — but it hasn't rained yet, so nobody knows.

Then winter arrives with the reveal. The first real storms find every seam that summer opened. Tule fog follows and makes it worse in a way outsiders underestimate: fog holds the roof surface at saturation for days at a time, giving water a standing invitation to wick into laps, blisters, and fastener holes that a brief rain would have run past. Freeze-adjacent fog nights pry openings wider. Meanwhile, wind-blown dust off the surrounding ag ground has spent the whole year washing into drains and low spots, damming water into ponds that sit exactly where the membrane is weakest.

That cycle — summer embrittlement, autumn dust load, winter saturation — is why Hanford roof leaks cluster in the first storms of the season, and why the smartest repair call is the one made in October, before the ceiling tile stains.

Rooftop equipment deserves its own line in the weather story. Evaporative coolers and package HVAC units are the hardest-working machines in Hanford, and their curbs, condensate lines, and service traffic are the most common leak origins we trace. A swamp cooler that's been overflowing quietly all summer will have soaked the assembly around its curb long before the first interior stain appears; a fall service visit that finds the overflow finds the leak before it becomes one. When we survey a roof, every piece of equipment gets photographed and its flashing condition logged — because that's where the water gets in.

Hail is rarer here than wind and dust, but Valley thunderstorms do occasionally throw it, and foam-and-coating systems with granule surfacing take it better than aged, brittle membranes. After any unusual weather event, a quick drone pass is cheap insurance — we can compare against the prior survey imagery on file and tell you in an afternoon whether anything actually changed.

Finding the Leak: Drone Survey and Core Sampling

Diagnosis is the half of leak repair that most repair outfits skip. Ours is systematic. The drone survey covers the entire roof field in high resolution — not just the area over the drip — because flat-roof water travel means the entry point is frequently a long way from the symptom. We're looking for the full catalog: open seams, split flashings, crushed foam around equipment stands, ponding rings, clogged drains and scuppers, fastener back-out patterns on metal, blistering on built-up surfaces.

Where the surface story is ambiguous, core sampling settles it. A core tells us what the assembly actually is — Hanford's older downtown buildings near Courthouse Square routinely carry three generations of roofing stacked up — and whether the insulation is wet. That distinction drives everything: a dry assembly with a local breach is a repair; a saturated field is a different conversation, honestly had. Moisture that stays trapped under a cosmetic patch doesn't just persist — it corrodes decks, grows mold, destroys R-value, and turns a four-figure repair into a six-figure replacement a few years out.

Everything we find goes into a written bid within 48 hours: photos, findings, scope, and numbers. No mystery, no verbal drift, no "while we're up here" surprises.

On larger or repeatedly patched roofs we add moisture mapping to the survey — reading the thermal signature of wet insulation across the field so the cut-and-dry scope is drawn around what's actually wet instead of guessed at. On a 30,000-square-foot processing roof, the difference between "replace it all" and "replace the 1,800 wet feet" is the difference between a capital project and a maintenance ticket, and the map is what proves it.

Flat Roof Repair Across Built-Up, Modified, and Single-Ply Systems

Flat roof repair in Hanford means being fluent in every system the last sixty years installed. Built-up gravel roofs on older civic and commercial buildings fail by alligatoring, blistering, and flashing splits at parapets — we repair with compatible plies and mastics, then talk honestly about whether a silicone restoration should follow. Modified bitumen fails at end laps and around whatever equipment was added after the roof went on — repairs are torch-or-cold-applied to match the system. Single-ply TPO and EPDM fail at seams, punctures, and shrinkage-stressed corners — we heat-weld or seam-repair with manufacturer-matched materials rather than smearing caulk over a membrane problem.

The pattern across all three: match the repair to the system, fix the detail that failed, and never bury moisture. Mismatched patch materials are their own failure mode — asphalt products attacking single-ply chemistry, incompatible mastics letting go in the first hot week — and Hanford roofs are full of them. Bringing in a crew that actually stocks and knows the right materials is the difference between a repair and a delay.

Parapet walls and coping are the sleeper failure on Hanford's older commercial blocks. Downtown buildings carry masonry parapets whose caps crack, whose counterflashings loosen, and whose walls drink fog moisture from both faces all winter. Water entering a parapet shows up as a wall stain a story below, gets diagnosed as a roof leak, and defeats three roof patches before somebody finally looks at the coping. We look at the coping first on any perimeter leak — and the repair menu runs from re-sealing and counterflashing up to a fluid-applied wrap of the entire parapet.

Foam Roof Repair: Blisters, Bird Pecks, and Impact Damage

Foam roof repair is our home turf — we install SPF, so we repair it with the fluency of the people who build it. SPF roofs in Kings County are tough, but they take damage in recognizable ways: impact gouges from dropped tools or wind-flung debris, bird pecking (a real Valley phenomenon, especially near ag operations where birds work the roofline), blisters where a past applicator sprayed over damp substrate, and topcoat wear-through on roofs that missed their recoat window.

The repairs are straightforward when done right: cut out damaged or wet foam back to sound material, dry the area, re-foam to grade, and re-coat with silicone tied into the surrounding field. Small punctures get sealant-and-mesh treatment; larger areas get the full cut-and-refoam. What kills foam roofs isn't damage — it's neglected damage, because exposed foam ages under UV. Caught early, a foam repair is one of the cheapest fixes in commercial roofing; our complete procedures are documented on the foam roof repair page. Kept on a recoat schedule afterward, the roof's lifespan keeps extending — see how long does spray foam roofing last for what that curve looks like over decades.

The most satisfying calls are the near-misses: a foam roof with the topcoat worn to yellow in the traffic lanes but the foam still sound underneath. Caught in that window, the fix is a cleaning, spot repairs, and a full silicone recoat at $2–$4 per square foot — the roof effectively restarts its clock. Missed by a few more years of UV, the same roof needs field re-foaming at several times the cost. If your Hanford foam roof is showing color through the coating, call this season, not next.

Leak Repair That Ends at the Cause, Not the Symptom

Leak repair earns its name only when the water stops for good, and that requires refusing two temptations. The first is patching the stain: tracing interior drips to the actual breach takes drone imagery, moisture logic, and sometimes cores — but a patch over the wrong spot is money spent to keep leaking. The second is patching over saturation: if the insulation around a breach is wet, sealing the top traps the problem in the assembly, where it quietly destroys the deck and R-value until the roof fails wholesale.

Our leak repairs in Hanford therefore always include the boring parts: verifying the entry point, checking the moisture halo around it, drying or removing wet material, and repairing with system-matched materials at the detail that failed — seam, flashing, penetration boot, pitch pan, drain bowl, or field breach. Then we document what we did and photograph it, because the next manager of that building deserves to know what's under the surface. It's the difference between a company that sells patches and a company that ends leaks.

Documentation earns its keep when insurance is involved. Storm-damage claims on Kings County commercial buildings live or die on evidence — dated drone imagery, the photographed breach, moisture readings, a written scope. Our repair reports are built to serve as that evidence: what failed, why, what it took to fix, and the condition of the surrounding field. Adjusters get answers instead of adjectives, claims move faster, and you're never reconstructing the story of your own roof from memory inside a claims portal.

Ponding Water: The Repair That's Really a Drainage Fix

Ponding is Hanford's signature roof disease. The town is table-flat, the roofs were built to match, and decades of settling plus dust accumulation leave most older flat roofs with birdbaths that hold water for days after rain — or weeks in fog season, when evaporation nearly stops. Standing water accelerates every failure mode: it soaks into aged asphalt, works seams, breeds algae that holds moisture even longer, and concentrates load on the deck's weakest framing.

Treating ponding as a leak problem misses the point; it's a geometry problem. Our fix menu runs from cheap to structural: clearing and rebuilding drain fields and scuppers that ag dust has dammed; installing overflow paths where original drainage was underbuilt; and — the tool most repair contractors don't have — spraying polyurethane foam crickets and slope build-ups that reshape the deck so water moves to the drains. Because spray polyurethane foam is fully adhered and self-flashing, it re-slopes a roof without the dead-load and cost of tapered board systems. Coated in silicone, the corrected areas are also the most waterproof parts of the roof.

On parapeted roofs the drains are only half the drainage; the overflow scuppers are the insurance policy. We find them dust-dammed, tarred over by past patchers, or missing entirely on older buildings. Part of every ponding repair is verifying that if the primary drain clogs during a storm, the water on your roof has a designed exit that isn't your ceiling.

Repair, Restore, or Replace: The Honest Decision Tree

Not every damaged roof should be repaired — and not every aging roof should be replaced. The decision logic we walk Hanford owners through: if damage is local and the assembly is dry, repair — it's the cheapest correct answer, and most calls end here. If the surface is broadly aged but the assembly is sound and dry, restore — a silicone coating system at $2–$4 per square foot resets the roof's clock for a fraction of replacement. If cores show widespread saturation, deck damage, or a roof past its structural life, replace — usually with SPF at $4–$8 per square foot, which installs over the prepared substrate without full tear-off in most cases; the complete economics live on our spray foam roofing cost page.

The point of the free assessment is that you get this decision made with data — drone imagery and core results — instead of with whichever answer the contractor happened to be selling. We install and repair every option on that tree, so we have no incentive to push you up it.

Timing the decision matters as much as making it. Kings County's repair season is compressed — everyone calls in the same three storm weeks — while October is quiet and fog season is predictable. Owners who inspect in early fall choose their repair path on their own calendar and budget cycle; owners who wait for the drip choose from whatever's possible mid-storm. The free assessment costs the same in October as in January, and it buys a very different set of options.

Metal Roof Leak Repair on Shops, Gins, and Warehouses

Kings County's steel buildings deserve their own repair playbook. Metal roof leaks almost never come through the panel; they come through the details — fastener holes where gaskets died in the heat, lap seams that opened under thermal cycling, ridge and eave closures that dust and fog corrosion ate, and rust-through where condensation sat along the purlin lines. Our metal repairs re-secure or replace backed-out fasteners with oversized gasketed screws, seal laps and closures with metal-rated sealants and mesh, convert surface rust before it perforates, and — where the detail failures are roof-wide — quote the honest upgrade: a full silicone restoration that seals every fastener and lap under one monolithic coat, or SPF over the panels for insulation and waterproofing in one pass.

Fog-season condensation drip inside metal buildings gets misdiagnosed as leaking constantly. If your "leak" appears on cold clear mornings without rain, it's condensation — an insulation problem, not a breach — and the fix is on our commercial insulation page, not a tube of caulk.

Skylights and ridge vents round out the metal-building leak census. Aged fiberglass panel skylights craze, crack under wind-flung debris, and leak at their side laps; ridge vents lose closures to wind and birds. We repair or replace panel skylights in kind, re-seal ridge details, and — where a building has collected a patchwork of both problems — fold them into a restoration scope so the whole roofline gets one service life instead of a dozen little ones.
